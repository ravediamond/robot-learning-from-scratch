# Model answers

1. Sparse: cheap (label the last frame of each episode, or train a classifier on a few hundred frames), unbiased about HOW the task is done, but gives no gradient until the first success: needs demos or interventions. Dense: speeds up learning and credit assignment, but must be engineered or learned, and any error in its shape becomes behaviour (the agent maximises the reward, not the task).
2. The agent searches for states the reward model likes. A false positive is a reward source that is easier to reach than real success, so the policy converges on it. False negatives only slow learning. So precision first: a high threshold, several consecutive positive frames, and negatives mined from the policy's own rollouts.
3. Later-is-better only holds along one successful trajectory. Across episodes starts, goals and speeds differ, so time order is not progress order. Cross-trajectory comparisons are valid when the pair shares the task and you know their relative quality (success vs failure, ranked or preference-labelled trajectories): Robometer trains on such trajectory comparisons to get a reward that generalises across tasks and embodiments.
4. The policy found states that fool the classifier (the spurious cue: for example the gripper closed in front of the camera). Fix: collect the exploiting rollouts, label them negative, retrain (adversarial/iterative negatives, as VICE or HIL-SERL do); raise the threshold; require the success signal for k consecutive steps; add a second view or proprioceptive checks; keep a human-verified success rate as the reported metric.
5. (a) Zero-shot VLM progress estimation on shuffled frames (GVL-style): no training, but noisy, slow, and weak on fine contact states. (b) Similarity between a video/JEPA embedding and a goal image or text: cheap and dense, but measures appearance, not task state. (c) A fine-tuned success or value head on a VLM backbone: accurate in-distribution, needs labelled robot data and exploits appear out of distribution.
6. Offline: held-out precision/recall at the chosen threshold; rank correlation with human progress labels; value-order consistency on successful episodes; scores on failure episodes and on near-miss negatives; counterfactual tests on suspected shortcuts. Then a short closed-loop pilot: watch what the policy converges to, and compare the model's success calls with human calls on every evaluation episode.
