# Model answers

1. L = −E[min(ρA, clip(ρ, 1−ε, 1+ε)A)], ρ = π_new/π_old. A > 0: raising ρ helps, but beyond 1+ε the clipped term is smaller and constant, so the gradient vanishes: no reward for moving further. A < 0: lowering ρ helps, but below 1−ε the clipped term is the smaller one and constant. In the harmful directions (ρ < 1 with A > 0, ρ > 1 with A < 0) the unclipped term is the minimum, so the full gradient pushes back.
2. It fixes the scale of the policy gradient whatever the reward scale, so one learning rate and one clip work across tasks. It can hurt with tiny minibatches (noisy mean and std), and when all advantages are nearly equal (it amplifies noise); it also changes the sign of some advantages by subtracting the mean.
3. Observations, actions, log π_old(a|s), values, rewards, dones. log π_old must come from the policy that sampled the action: recomputing it after an update gives ratio = 1 always and removes the trust region. Recomputed values change the advantages during the update.
4. Simulators give thousands of parallel environments at 10⁴–10⁶ steps per second, with free resets and no wear. A real arm gives about 10–30 steps per second, needs resets and supervision, and exploration noise is unsafe. On-policy data is thrown away after each update, which only a simulator can afford.
5. (1) Learning rate or clip too large, or too many epochs: look at the approximate KL per update and the clip fraction. (2) Advantage or return bugs (done handling, bootstrap). (3) The log-std collapsing or exploding: log the entropy. (4) Value loss scale swamping the policy loss, or unnormalised observations and rewards. Then seeds: PPO varies a lot across seeds.
6. (a) PPO: massive parallel simulation makes sample efficiency irrelevant, and PPO is stable and simple to scale. (b) An off-policy method (SAC/TD3-style with demonstrations and interventions, as in HIL-SERL) or offline/advantage-conditioned improvement: every real transition is expensive, so reuse the data many times.
