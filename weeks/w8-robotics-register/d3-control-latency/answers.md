# Model answers

1. P: push proportional to the error (fast, leaves offset under load). I: accumulate error to remove steady offsets (gravity, friction), risk of overshoot/windup. D: react to the error's rate, damping. The SO-101's Feetech servos run their own position loop at kHz rates; your policy sends position SETPOINTS at 30 Hz, so the policy is an outer loop on top of the servo's inner loop.
2. With delay, the controller reacts to old errors: by the time a correction lands, the state has moved; high gain over-corrects, the next delayed correction overshoots the other way: oscillation. Same at the policy level: 300 ms inference means acting on a 300 ms old picture.
3. Typical: naive lags (tracking error several times higher), shift removes the lag but keeps jumps from offset changes, blend reduces max jump. RTC goes further: it generates the new chunk CONDITIONED on the actions that will be executed during inference (inpainting with a frozen prefix and guidance), so the new chunk is consistent by construction, not just cross-faded; it also handles multimodal switches.
4. The derivative is computed per step instead of per second, so its gain implicitly scales with dt²: changing the rate changes the effective kd. Lesson: write control code in physical units (per second), keep dt explicit, and test with a known signal (ramp → known derivative).
5. t: capture image + state → transfer → inference (~d steps) → chunk arrives at t + d → skip/blend the stale part → execute exec_h actions at 30 Hz → while executing, request the next chunk early enough (request when remaining ≥ d) → repeat.
6. Async inference thread; request the next chunk when the queue drops to ~d + margin actions (d ≈ 9–10 steps at 30 Hz); time-index chunks (shift mode); boundary consistency via RTC-style prefix freezing (or blending as a fallback); a low-level smoother and safety limits (velocity/acceleration clamps) in the 30 Hz loop; log timestamps to measure real d. Then cut d: fewer flow steps, cached prefix, quantisation, vla.cpp.
