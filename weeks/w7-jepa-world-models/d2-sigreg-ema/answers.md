# Model answers

1. Cramér–Wold: a distribution is determined by all its 1-D projections; with enough random directions, any deviation from N(0, I) (wrong mean, wrong scale, correlations, collapse, heavy tails) shows up in some projections. It can miss deviations confined to directions the sample of projections did not cover (few directions, high d), and a finite test can only detect what the sample size allows.
2. An isotropic Gaussian latent has no collapse, no redundant dimensions, a known scale, and a smooth density without holes: distances and linear probes are better behaved, sampling and planning are easier. LeJEPA argues the isotropic Gaussian is the optimal embedding distribution for downstream prediction risk.
3. Stop-gradient: the target is a fixed regression target for this step (no co-adaptation toward the constant). EMA: the target changes slowly and smoothly, giving a stable but improving target. Stop-gradient alone with target = online (SimSiam-like) can work with a predictor but is fragile; EMA without stop-gradient lets gradients flow into the target path and collapse returns.
4. 99.6% new (the online weights) and only 0.4% kept: the target IS the online network, one step behind: no slow target, nearly symmetric training, collapse.
5. Each hyperparameter multiplies the tuning cost, and multi-term losses with interacting weights are fragile across datasets and scales: a setting that works on one dataset collapses on another. One λ is cheap to sweep and transfers, which matters with one GPU and limited time.
6. Collapse = the constant solution. Families: (1) asymmetry, stop-grad + EMA target (BYOL, I-JEPA, V-JEPA); (2) regularise batch statistics (VICReg); (3) match a target distribution (SIGReg, LeWM); plus frozen pretrained encoders (DINO-WM). On an SO-101 with one GPU: SIGReg/LeWM style: end-to-end from pixels, one hyperparameter, small model, trains in hours: what you already ran.
