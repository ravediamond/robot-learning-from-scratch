# Model answers

1. Consecutive frames are near-duplicates; a frame split puts neighbors on both sides, so val measures memorization. Evidence line: "on a frame split, a nearest-neighbor lookup looks several times better than on an episode split; the dumbest policy wins". (Quote your actual numbers from `predict_run.py`.)
2. (a) Compounding error: BC is trained on the operator's state distribution; each small error moves the robot to states never seen in training, where errors are larger, and they grow over the episode (Ross & Bagnell, DAgger). Val MSE is measured on the operator's states, so it cannot see this. (b) Val MSE averages all steps equally, but success depends on a few critical steps (grasp). (c) Closed-loop effects: latency, servo tracking, contact, the gripper. Also the cost of a wrong mode is not captured by MSE.
3. Absolute targets: no drift (errors do not integrate), and they match how the servos are commanded. Deltas: invariant to the absolute position, often generalize better across poses and are smaller numbers; but errors accumulate. Many VLAs use deltas or relative chunks for end-effector control and absolute for joints.
4. Joints have different scales (pan ±150°, gripper 0–100): in raw MSE the large-range joints dominate the loss and the model spends capacity there. In normalized space each dim counts the same. The gripper is critical for success and has few "events" (closing): in raw units its error may be swamped.
5. (1) Reload and compare outputs with the in-memory model on a fixed input (exact). (2) `load_state_dict(strict=True)` and count tensors loaded vs expected; print missing/unexpected keys. (3) Check normalization stats are present and equal to the training ones. (4) Check the config: action dim order, units (deg vs rad), absolute vs delta, chunk size. (5) Run the policy on a recorded training episode open-loop: actions should track the recorded ones. Only then blame the policy.
6. MSE regresses to the mean: it averages left and right and drives straight into the obstacle. Week 4: generative heads (diffusion / flow matching) model the full action distribution and sample one mode; ACT's CVAE and chunking (commit to one mode per chunk) also help.
7. Cheapest baseline: replay the mean demo trajectory open-loop, or a scripted pick for a fixed layout, or "do nothing" (0%), plus the previous policy. Val MSE is a proxy; the claim that matters is success rate, which is a binomial count: with 20 trials, 14/20 vs 10/20 is not a clear difference. Need a CI and a pre-registered bar (chapter 7: Wilson interval, episode bootstrap).
8. Decide the rule before looking: e.g. last checkpoint, or best val on a smoothed curve with a fixed window; then confirm on the robot with a fixed small set of trials for the top 2, with a CI. Never pick the best of many checkpoints on the same trials you report (that is selection on noise; report on fresh trials).
9. (a) Operator noise: 0.5° random per step, unpredictable by construction. (b) The operator's gain differs per episode and is not in the observation: from one frame, the policy cannot know how aggressive this operator is, so it predicts the average. More epochs only overfit. Fix: give it history (2–3 past states reveal the gain), i.e. a temporal context, which is exactly why policies use observation history or chunks. Model vs data test: an oracle that sees the hidden variable (or a much bigger model) gives the floor; if a bigger model and longer training do not move it, the limit is in the data/observation, not the model.
