# Model answers

1. (a) Token count: H × D tokens per chunk decoded one by one (300 forward passes of a big LM for one chunk) is too slow for 30–50 Hz. (b) Redundancy and poor credit assignment: consecutive tokens are nearly identical for smooth motion, so next-token prediction becomes trivially "copy the previous" and the model learns little; precision is capped by bin width.
2. Smooth signals are well approximated by a few low-frequency cosines (energy compaction), so most high coefficients are near 0 and vanish after quantization. Discontinuities (gripper open/close steps, contact events) spread energy across all frequencies (Gibbs effect): truncation causes ringing around the step.
3. Fixed uniform bins: simple, data-independent, waste resolution where the data never goes. Quantile bins (OpenVLA uses 1st–99th percentile range per dim): resolution where the data is, but depend on the dataset statistics (another normalization artifact to keep in sync), and clip rare extremes.
4. Width = 300°/256 = 1.17°, bias = 0.59° per joint, on every step. In val loss: a small constant offset, easy to miss among other errors; on the robot: a consistent placement error of a few mm at the gripper. Seen if you plot mean signed error per joint.
5. With 200 demos at 30 Hz: flow head (π0/SmolVLA style): continuous precision, chunk in ~10 parallel steps, proven on SO-100/101 data; FAST tokens shine with large-scale co-training with language (π0-FAST trains faster) and when reusing an autoregressive LM as-is. Answer with latency and precision requirements.
6. The gripper is nearly binary with sharp transitions: 2–3 bins or a separate binary token (open/close), not part of the DCT (steps compress badly). Some systems predict it with a classification head and the joints with a continuous head.
7. At the ends of the chunk. The DCT-II implicitly extends the signal by mirroring it: continuous, but the slope flips sign at the boundary, which is a kink whose spectrum decays slowly (~1/k²). Truncation leaves the error concentrated at the endpoints, exactly where the next chunk takes over: one more reason to care about chunk boundaries.
