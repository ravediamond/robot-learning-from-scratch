# Model answers

1. Hardware: servo torque and current limits, mechanical stops, a hardware e-stop that cuts power. Control: the safety filter (limits, rate limits, watchdog, NaN guard), velocity and force limits in the low-level controller. Monitoring: failure and anomaly detectors that stop or slow the policy, workspace monitoring (keep-out zones, people detection). Process: trained operator with the e-stop, reduced speed near people, risk assessment of the cell, procedures for resets and incidents.
2. Simple, fast, checkable rules that hold for every policy: limits, rates, staleness, finiteness, latches, keep-out zones in joint or Cartesian space. Not: learned components or judgments that need context (is this grasp going to fail?); those belong to monitors whose output feeds a simple rule ("stop if the monitor says so").
3. The condition that triggered it may clear by itself (a transient sensor glitch) while the reason is unknown; restarting automatically could restart a dangerous motion. A deliberate human reset after checking the scene, logged, is required.
4. Capture (image and joint read), receive, inference start and end, command sent, and the servo's acknowledgement if available, all on one clock. From them: observation age at decision time, inference latency, end-to-end latency, jitter, and the action-state lag; they are what you need to debug "it did something weird".
5. Holding a position under load (an arm holding a heavy object) can be fine, but holding mid-motion near a person or during contact (pressing on a surface) may keep applying force; and for a mobile base, "hold the last velocity" is dangerous. The safe action is task and robot dependent: decelerate to stop, relax compliance, or retract to a safe pose.
6. Unit tests for every rule and its edges, property-based tests (output always within limits, step size always ≤ max, never non-finite, for random inputs), fault-injection runs on the real system (unplug the camera, inject NaNs, delay frames) with logs proving the response, and code review of every change; keep the code small enough to read in one sitting.
