# Model answers

1. BC is trained only on states the expert visits; one mistake or push moves the robot off that distribution, where the policy's errors are larger, which moves it further off: errors feed on themselves. Numbers: BC ~0.4 success under pushes, DAgger ~1.0 after 1–2 iterations, paired: ~110 starts DAgger-only vs ~0 BC-only.
2. 60 expert demos (6× more labels than 10 demos, more than DAgger adds) barely help: they all come from the same undisturbed upper path, so the post-push states are still never seen. DAgger adds fewer but RIGHT labels: the states the policy actually reaches. Coverage beats volume.
3. A human teleoperator: they watch the policy, take over when it goes wrong (HG-DAgger / intervention), and the takeover segments are labelled recovery data. Cheaper variants: deliberately perturb the arm during teleop (start from off-nominal poses), your forward/undo pairs, scripted resets that start from failure states. Imperfect experts: weight or filter corrections, only use intervention segments.
4. Mostly wandering/timeouts (the policy outputs weak or inconsistent actions in unseen states), few collisions. A collision detector alone would miss most failures; you need progress/time-based monitors (the length null!).
5. Self-labels: the policy is trained to reproduce its own behaviour on the new states, which it already does. The loss on those states is ~0; nothing changes; dataset size grows, success does not.
6. Large and diverse data that includes mistakes and recoveries (many operators, autonomous rollouts with human corrections, data collected on deployed robots), co-training, and post-training on high-quality and correction data (π0.5 / π*-style RL from experience, "recap"). The principle is DAgger's: data from the policy's own state distribution, labelled with better actions.
