# Model answers

1. "If I pick one failed run and one successful run at random, AUROC is the chance the monitor rates the failed one as more alarming." Not a deployment decision: deployment needs a threshold, and the cost of false alarms (interrupting good runs) vs misses, plus how early the alarm comes.
2. Lengths are integers (and often capped at a timeout), so many episodes share a score. Arbitrary tie-breaking by row order turns ties into wins or losses for one class, so AUROC depends on the table's ordering; failures hitting the same timeout are a big tie group.
3. Guaranteed: for a new episode exchangeable with the calibration successes, P(false alarm) ≤ α, marginally (on average over calibration sets). Assumption: exchangeability (same distribution, no drift). Not guaranteed: any detection rate on failures (that depends on the score's quality), conditional coverage per scenario, validity under distribution shift.
4. Report your number. "Beats" = paired comparison on the same episodes: bootstrap the AUROC DIFFERENCE over episodes (or DeLong test), with a pre-registered margin; overlapping marginal CIs are not the test. Within-policy strata if several policies are mixed, as in your paper.
5. Recovery needs lead time: an alarm at the failure moment leaves nothing to fix (the bowl is already dropped). Measure detection time relative to failure onset; a lower-AUROC monitor that fires 1 s earlier can be more useful.
6. Data: labelled rollouts per arm/policy/task with failure onsets, episode-level splits across arms and days. Baselines: elapsed time, action variance, policy confidence, previous monitor. Metrics: AUROC with episode bootstrap, TPR at a fixed FPR set by the cost of interruptions, median lead time, per-arm breakdown. Bars pre-registered (e.g. +0.05 AUROC over the length null, paired; FPR ≤ 5% conformal-calibrated per arm). Then a live A/B: success rate with and without monitor-triggered recovery.
