# Model answers

1. Schema and timestamps (monotonic, fps, gaps: W2 D4), frozen or black cameras, dead or noisy joints, limit hits and out-of-range values, calibration consistency, action-state lag, idle head and tail, length outliers, duplicates, task-label sanity (a "pick" episode where the gripper never closes), operator and station metadata present. Each protects the policy from learning a behaviour that is not the task: freezing (frozen image), ignoring a joint, pushing into limits, waiting, or memorising duplicates.
2. Mean and sd are not robust: a single extreme value moves the mean and inflates the sd, so other outliers fall inside the threshold (masking), and the extreme one itself scores lower than it should. Median and MAD have a 50% breakdown point: up to half the data can be arbitrary before they move. 0.6745 rescales MAD to the sd of a normal distribution, so 3.5 is comparable to a classical threshold.
3. Quarantine: keep the data, exclude it from training sets by default, with the reason and the check version recorded in a ledger. Deletion destroys evidence (the station's camera problem) and makes the decision irreversible. A data owner reviews quarantine reports regularly; promotions back into training are logged like any other dataset change.
4. Compare per-session statistics of each joint's position at a fixed reference pose (start/rest pose), or of the joint-to-camera consistency (forward kinematics of the end effector vs its detected position): a step change between sessions that persists is a recalibration. Monitor the distribution of state at episode start per station over time (change-point detection); record calibration file hashes in the episode metadata so the change is visible directly.
5. Next hour: look at a sample of the flagged episodes and their raw data, confirm it is a real defect (not a bad threshold), stop or warn that station's collection, notify its operator. Next week: root cause (hardware, USB bandwidth, firmware, procedure), fix, re-validate the station with a short recording, decide whether the affected data is salvageable (trim the bad segments) or stays quarantined, and add a live check at recording time so it is caught in minutes next time.
6. Seed known defects (as `fake_fleet` does) and real ones from incidents, measure recall per check; have a human review a random sample of passed and flagged episodes to estimate precision. A false positive throws away paid-for data and, worse, can bias the dataset (if a check flags fast operators, you lose a style). Track the flag rate per station and operator over time.
